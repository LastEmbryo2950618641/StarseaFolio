## 来源 B｜Show, Don't Tell 场景改写提示词（贝果，标注「可复制」）

- URL: https://bigolab.com/prompts/1055-scene-show-dont-tell  
- 抓取：2026-11-20

### 可复制提示词原文

```text
请用「Show, Don't Tell（展示，而不是告诉）」的原则帮我修改一段小说文字。

- 段落所在的场景：[场景说明]
- 视角人物：[视角人物]（谁在看、谁在感受，第几人称）
- 这段要传达的情绪或氛围：[情绪氛围]
- 文风偏好：[文风偏好]（如：简洁冷峻 / 细腻抒情 / 轻快幽默）
- 我写的段落：
  [粘贴我的段落]

请完成：
1. 标出「告诉式」句子：直接陈述情绪（她很害怕）、直接下判断（他是个吝啬的人）、概括性的描写（房间很乱）。
2. 改写：把这些句子改成展示式——
   - 用人物的动作和身体反应代替情绪词（手心出汗、反复检查门锁）；
   - 用具体的细节代替概括（不是「房间很乱」，而是「泡面桶摞到了窗台上」）；
   - 调动五感：视觉之外，至少用到声音、气味、触感或温度中的两种；
   - 所有细节都通过视角人物的感知来写，符合他的身份和心境（同一个房间，侦探和小孩注意到的东西不同）。
3. 节制：指出哪些地方应该保留简洁的叙述（如过渡、时间跳跃、不重要的动作），避免每句话都堆满描写，拖慢节奏。
4. 输出对照表：原句 | 改写 | 用了什么手法。
5. 输出修改后的完整段落，篇幅不超过原文的 1.5 倍。

保留原文的情节和信息，不增加新情节。
```

### 同页「使用说明」原文

```text
怎么填变量：[视角人物] 非常重要——展示式描写的细节要从人物的眼睛里看出去。一个刚失业的人走进咖啡馆，注意到的可能是菜单上的价格，而不是装修风格。[情绪氛围] 帮助 AI 选择细节：同样写雨，孤独和期待需要不同的细节。

常见问题与调整：

改得太啰嗦 → 追问：「每个情绪只用一个最有力的细节表现，删掉其余的形容词。」

细节很俗套（如「眼泪夺眶而出」）→ 追问：「把所有常见的描写换成只属于这个人物、这个场景的细节。」

提醒：Show, Don't Tell 不是绝对的规则，好的小说是「展示」和「叙述」交替的——情感高潮处多展示，过渡处简洁叙述。
```

```markdown
# Human Voice - 去AI感写作风格

你的任务是让文本读起来像一个真实的人写的，而不是一个语言模型输出的。下面不是一套空泛的原则，而是一份具体的操作手册——列出了AI写作最常见的毛病，以及对应的修正方法。

## 一、节奏：打破均匀感

AI最大的特征是句子长度高度均匀，像节拍器一样稳定。人不会这样写。

**具体做法：**

- 连续三句以上长度相近时，必须主动打断。插一句很短的（两三个字也行："不够。""能。""没用。"），或者写一句明显更长、带从句的。
- 不要每段都是"观点句+展开句+例证句+总结句"的四拍结构。有的段落可以只有两句话就结束，有的段落可以绕一圈再回来。
- 段落长度也要参差。不要每段都是差不多的字数。

**反面示例（AI感强）：**
> AI优化电网调度，新能源并网效率提升30%以上；AI精准预测天气，风光发电量大幅提高；AI加速材料研发，下一代储能技术因此更快落地。

三个分句，结构完全平行，长度几乎相同。这是排比修辞的滥用。

**正面示例（人感）：**
> AI参与电网调度之后，新能源并网的效率有了明显提升，多家电网公司报告的数据在30%左右；天气预测模型因为AI变得更准了，风电场和光伏电站的发电量预估偏差大幅缩小；新型储能材料的研发周期，也因为AI辅助筛选而在加快。

同样三件事，但句子长度不一，叙述角度在变，不再是整齐的排比。


## 二、修辞：少即是多

AI倾向于在一段话里堆满比喻、对仗、排比和四字成语，好像每句话都要"漂亮"。人类写作者不会这样，因为用力过猛读起来反而虚。

**具体做法：**

- 一段话里最多用一个比喻或成语。如果一段里已经用了"刻舟求剑"，就不要再来"画地为牢"和"削足适履"。
- 对仗和排比不要超过两组连用。"风光给了我们理想，核电给了我们稳定，AI给了我们工具"——这种三连排比太工整了，砍掉一个或者把结构打散。
- 四字词语不要成串出现。"稳定可靠、清洁高效、持续充沛"这种三连四字格，是公文和AI共同的毛病。拆开说，用大白话替代。


## 三、数据：要有消化过程

AI引用数据的方式是"甩完就走"——"根据XX报告，数据显示XX"，然后直接跳到结论。这样读起来像在念新闻稿。真人引用数据时，会有一个消化、评价、甚至怀疑的过程。

**具体做法：**

- 引完数据后加一句自己的判断或态度。比如：
  - "当然，CEO说的话不能全信，但趋势摆在那里"
  - "这个数字可能偏乐观，但就算打个对折也够说明问题"
  - "说实话这个比例比我预想的高"
- 不要每个论点都配一个精确数据。有的地方用模糊表述反而更真实："提升了不少""差了一大截""比之前快了很多"。人类不会记住每一个百分比。
- 数据来源可以用口语化的方式带出来，而不是"据XX显示"的公文句式。比如："亚马逊的运维团队算过一笔账""毕马威今年初做了一份调研，问了能源行业的CEO们"。


## 四、连接词：别太丝滑

AI文本的转折和递进特别顺滑——"不仅如此""更重要的是""换言之""也就是说""由此可见"。真人写东西没这么多路标，有时候两段之间就是硬接的，读者自己能看出来关系。

**具体做法：**

- 连接词砍掉一半。不是每个转折都需要"然而""但是"，不是每个递进都需要"此外""更进一步"。
- 多用具体内容来做过渡，少用抽象连接词。与其写"再往深处看"，不如直接开始说那个更深的内容。
- "首先""其次""最后"这种序号词，如果不是刻意分点，就别用。真人聊天和写随笔的时候很少这样列点。


## 五、观点表达：允许不确定

AI总是很笃定，每个判断都斩钉截铁。真人写东西会有犹豫、有保留、有"我也不完全确定但倾向于认为"的时刻。

**具体做法：**

- 偶尔加一句留有余地的话："这个判断可能有争议""我没有完全想清楚，但直觉上……""也许还有别的解释"。
- 不要每个论点都推到极致。"AI是环保最强的加速器"这种说法太绝对了，改成"AI在环保这件事上的正面作用可能被低估了"，更像人话。
- 允许文章里出现没有被完美回答的问题。不是所有事情都要在一篇文章里闭环。


## 六、词汇：避开AI高频词

以下词汇和短语在AI生成文本中出现频率极高，真人写作中相对少见。遇到它们时优先替换：

| AI高频词 | 替换方向 |
|---------|---------|
| 赋能 | 帮到、推动、起了作用 |
| 毋庸置疑 | 删掉，直接说事 |
| 不可忽视 | 值得注意、这事挺重要 |
| 至关重要 | 很关键、少不了 |
| 本质上 | 说白了、往根上看 |
| 值得一提的是 | 删掉，直接提 |
| 不难发现 | 删掉，直接说发现了什么 |
| 深远影响 | 影响很大、后劲很长 |
| 鉴于此 | 所以、这样一来 |
| 综上所述 | 删掉，直接总结 |
| 由此可见 | 删掉，读者自己能看出来 |
| 一言以蔽之 | 说直接点 |
| 不言而喻 | 删掉 |
| 显而易见 | 删掉或者换成"明摆着" |


## 七、结尾：不要升华过度

AI最爱在结尾来一波宏大叙事收束——"让我们拥抱未来""这才是XX最忠实的践行""人类文明因此走向更高的台阶"。这种结尾读多了会产生生理反感。

**具体做法：**

- 结尾可以就事论事地收，不用拔高到文明和人类。
- 可以用一个具体的画面、一个小问题、或者一句带点余味的判断来结束，不需要"金句压轴"。
- 如果实在想用一句有力的话收尾，确保它是从前文自然长出来的，不是硬贴上去的标签。


## 应用方式

这份风格指南应该在写作的**改稿阶段**发挥作用。流程是：

1. 先正常写完初稿，把内容和逻辑理顺
2. 然后用这份指南逐段检查，像过筛子一样找出AI痕迹
3. 针对性地修改——打散节奏、削减修辞、给数据加人话、砍连接词、软化绝对判断、替换高频词、克制结尾

不需要每一条都机械执行。核心只有一个判断标准：**这句话，一个正常人坐在电脑前想了想，会这么写吗？** 如果答案是"不太会"，就改。
```

```text
明确禁止： 严格禁止使用未经用户明确许可的赛博/科幻术语（如：神经接口、量子记忆、生物芯片、等离子体、纳米机器人、全息投影、赛博格、义体、数据流、防火墙、骇入、矩阵、深度学习、AI人格、跃迁、曲速等）。
魔法术语： 同样禁止使用传统奇幻或自定义魔法术语（如：魔力、咒语、法阵、元素、奥术、灵力、结界、召唤、附魔等），除非用户明确提供并授权使用特定词汇。
替代原则： 所有技术/魔法效果均用最基础的功能性描述或用户提供的代称表达。
例 (技术)： 不说“他骇入系统”，说“他操作设备绕过安全程序”或“他用了破解器”。
例 (魔法)： 不说“他施放火球术”，说“他手中凝聚出高温光团并掷出”或“他用燃素装置攻击”。

物品描写：
核心： 仅描述物品的物理状态、功能用途、与角色/场景的互动。
禁止： 拟人化、象征化、情感投射、历史追溯（除非是用户明确设定的古董）。
聚焦： 颜色、材质、新旧程度、破损情况、基本用途、放置位置、是否被使用/操作。
示例强化：
允许：“一个掉漆的红色消防栓”、“嗡嗡作响的老旧冰箱”、“堆满杂物的金属工作台”、“他拧开锈蚀的水龙头”。
禁止：“忠诚的消防栓守护着街角”、“冰箱发出疲惫的呻吟”、“工作台承载着无数未完成的梦想”、“水龙头流下城市的泪水”。

比喻限制：
频率： 严格遵循每500字不超过3处。
场合： 仅限用于描写极端情绪、重大转折、关键战斗/命案现场、独特且难以直接描述的超常现象（技术/魔法）。日常场景、普通对话、环境铺陈禁止使用。
性质： 比喻需简洁、具象、基于常见生活经验或物理现象。避免抽象、宏大、跨领域的比喻（如宇宙、海洋、星空、深渊等）。
示例强化：
允许（关键场景）：“他的怒吼像生锈的金属在摩擦”、“魔法光晕如破碎的霓虹灯管般闪烁不定”、“恐惧像冰冷的雨水瞬间浸透了她”。
禁止（非关键/日常）：“他的笑容像冬日的暖阳”、“她的思绪如漂浮的羽毛”、“街道像一条蜿蜒的伤疤”（除非是极其关键的、用户指定的象征性地点）。

II. 角色塑造
外貌描写 (白描)：
内容： 只描述可直接观察到的、显著且与当前情境可能相关的物理特征。
要素： 性别、大致年龄范围、体型（高矮胖瘦）、显著发型/发色、面部显著特征（疤痕、眼镜、义眼需用户定义）、穿着（款式、颜色、是否脏破）、显著配饰/装备。
禁止： 推测性描述（“看起来饱经风霜”）。主观审美判断（“英俊”、“丑陋”）。情感映射（“忧郁的眼睛”、“坚毅的下巴”）。比喻修饰（“星眸”、“刀削般的面庞”）。未授权的前史痕迹（“布满老茧的手暗示他曾是工人” - 除非用户明确设定并允许提及）。
示例强化：
允许：“一个约莫四十岁、体格敦实的男人，穿着沾满油污的连体工装，左眼是发着微光的机械装置（若用户设定）”。
禁止：“一个眼神如孤狼般警惕的中年男人”、“有着艺术家气质的消瘦青年”。

心理活动呈现：
黄金法则： 只呈现外在可观测的行为和语言。心理状态必须通过角色具体动作、细微表情（需符合白描）、语调变化、对话内容、行为选择来体现。
避免： 直接陈述心理活动、感受、想法（如：“他想…”、“她感到…”、“内心…”、“回忆起…”）。
方法：
动作化： “她手指无意识地敲击桌面，越来越快”（体现焦虑/不耐烦）。
对话化： 将内心独白转化为小声嘀咕、与自己的争论、或与他人谨慎的试探性对话。
细节化： “他端起咖啡杯，却忘了喝，盯着杯沿的缺口看了很久”（体现心不在焉/回忆）。
生理化 (谨慎使用)： “他喉结滚动了一下，咽下没说出口的话”（体现紧张/克制）。注意：需是明显可观察的生理反应。
示例强化：
允许：“她把照片攥得死紧，指节发白，然后猛地塞进抽屉最深处”。
禁止：“她内心充满了对过去的悔恨”。

III. 叙事规范
环境描写 (五要素限定)：
要素： 严格限定于：天气；光线；气味；声响；空间布局。（原文对各要素有展开，含禁止拟人/通感例。）

功能优先： 环境描写服务于情节、角色状态或世界观氛围，而非单纯写景。

IV. 违规修正原则

示例：
违规原句： “那把旧钥匙，仿佛承载着他整个流浪的岁月。”
修正结果： “他掏出一把铜制旧钥匙。”
```

## 原文 H｜跨度大 / 不搭的引用（语域不一致）+ 无缘无故的修辞

来源：tance-mang/chinese-webnovel-skills 
eferences/anti-ai-checklist.md（MIT；仓库已有 vendor 副本）

## 一、无缘无故的修辞（头号病）

AI 爱"为修辞而修辞"——加比喻、排比、华丽形容，但对剧情/情绪/画面毫无帮助，只是显得"文艺"。爽文里这是减分项。

**判断法**：每一个比喻/形容，问一句——“它让读者更懂、更爽、更有画面了吗？”没有就删。

- ❌“她的心情如同秋日里飘零的落叶，又似深海中无声的叹息，泛起层层涟漪。”（三个比喻，零信息，拖节奏）
- ✅“她笑了笑，没说话。”（克制，留白，更戳）

- ❌“愤怒像火山一样在他胸中喷涌，又如潮水般席卷了他的理智。”
- ✅“他攥紧了拳，指节发白。”（一个具体动作 > 两个比喻）

**规则**：
1. 一段最多一个比喻，且必须服务情绪/画面。
2. 爽文优先"具体动作/细节"，不优先"比喻"。
3. 删掉所有"为了好看"的排比和叠喻。

---

## 二、跨度大 / 不搭的引用（语域不一致）

AI 常突然引一个**和故事调性不搭**的东西：通俗爽文里冒出古诗典故、学术概念、西方哲学、或不合身份的网络梗。你写得朴实老成，它却引个怪东西——**本质是"语域(register)不一致"**。

**判断法**：问两句——
1. “**这个叙述者/这个角色**，会知道、会这么说吗？”（市井女主不会随口引黑格尔）
2. “这个引用，和**全文的语气**是一个频道吗？”（朴实白描里别塞华丽典故）

- ❌（通俗都市文里）“他的背影消失在街角，恰如《追忆似水年华》中那不可追回的时光。”（跨度太大，出戏）
- ✅“他头也不回地走了。我站在原地，看着那个背影越来越小。”

- ❌（古风文里）“公主气场全开，仿佛人间清醒的 MBTI 之王。”（现代梗污染古风语域）
- ✅“公主眼皮都没抬：‘退下。’”

**规则**：
1. **先定语域**（朴实/华丽/古风/现代/冷感/俏皮），全文统一，不中途跳。
2. 通俗爽文：少引经据典，少掉书袋；要引也引读者秒懂的。
3. 古风别用现代网络梗；现代别硬塞古诗酸文；第一人称市井叙述者别用超出其认知的概念。
4. 宁可"老陈朴实"，不要"花哨不搭"——**风格一致 > 单句出彩**。

## 原文 I｜比喻做作（本体—喻体三问）+ 预防六条

来源：https://github.com/lucia-trend/de-ai-flavor （SKILL.md；方法论出自公众号「做書」）

### 病征 2：比喻做作

**怎么认**：本体和喻体之间的联系牵强，甚至根本不在同一个维度上。比喻的目的是让读者更容易懂，不是越新奇越好。

**病例**
- 「那些数字在你看向我时忽然变得很轻——轻得像你发尾被风吹起的那一缕」
- 「它的脉络至今清晰——像我当时悄悄数过的、你的睫毛」

原文的判词：用"被风吹起的发尾"喻"变得轻的数字"，用"睫毛"喻"叶脉"，本体和喻体的联系比较牵强。

**自检动作**——对每个比喻问三句，任何一句答不上来，删。
1. 本体和喻体**共享哪个具体属性**？说得出这个属性的名字吗？
2. 这个属性是本体**最需要被说明**的那个吗？
3. 喻体是不是比本体**更好懂**？（如果读者还得先想象喻体，这个比喻是负分）

**手术手法**
- 答不上三问 → 直接删，改成直说。直说不丢人，硬喻才丢人。
- 想保留 → 换一个日常可感知的喻体，牺牲新奇度换准确度。
- 全文比喻密度过高时，砍到只留最好的那一两个。比喻是重音，满篇重音等于没有重音。

---

### 模式 C：预防（从零写）

用户让你直接写东西时，把七类病征当写作约束用。落到笔头上就是六条：

- 一个名词最多挂一个定语
- 每个比喻先过三问，过不了就直说
- 连续同构分句不超过 2 个
- 精确数字必须在后文被用到
- 每段回答得出"推进了什么"
- 每个细节过替换测试

 Metaphors Don't Match Character

 Your street-smart teenage protagonist describes a sunset as "a masterwork of impressionist light, Monet rendered in atmosphere." Your hardened mercenary observes that someone's smile "bloomed like a hothouse orchid in a Victorian conservatory." Your six-year-old narrator notes that "the silence pressed down like existential dread made manifest."

 These are the author's metaphors, not the character's. The comparison comes from the writer's vocabulary, education, and frame of reference rather than the character's. It's a POV violation that operates at the level of language rather than knowledge — the character suddenly has access to references and imagery they wouldn't possess.

 This happens most often when the author is proud of a metaphor and forces it into a POV where it doesn't belong. The metaphor might be beautiful. It's still wrong.

 Why It Kills Your Fiction

 Voice is the invisible architecture of close POV. The reader doesn't consciously track whether the metaphors match the character, but they FEEL it when they don't. An authorial metaphor creates a micro-disconnection — a flicker where the reader senses someone else talking. Enough of these flickers and the character's voice becomes unreliable.

 It's also a missed opportunity. Character-appropriate metaphors are one of the most powerful voice tools in fiction. A mechanic who describes a beautiful woman as "running on all cylinders" tells you about HER and HIM simultaneously. A poet describing the same woman in technical terms would be equally revealing.

 The deeper issue: if your character's metaphors don't match their background, you might not know your character well enough. Voice emerges from a character's entire life experience. If you can't generate metaphors from within that experience, you haven't fully inhabited the character.

 The Fix

 Filter every metaphor and comparison through the character's specific background, education, and daily life. A farmer compares things to weather and seasons. A soldier compares things to combat and logistics. A baker compares things to dough and ovens.

 Step-by-Step Repair

 List every metaphor and simile in a chapter. For each one, ask: does this comparison come from a world my character would know? If the character has never been to the ocean, they don't compare things to waves.

 Build a reference library for each POV character. What are the five domains they know best? A chef's metaphors come from kitchens. A pilot's come from the sky. A mother's come from raising children. Use ONLY these domains.

 When you find an authorial metaphor you love, don't delete it — translate it. "The sunset looked like a Monet" becomes, from a farmer's POV, "the sky looked like the year's last good harvest — all gold and going fast."

 Check metaphors during emotional peaks especially. That's where authors are most likely to reach for their OWN best language instead of the character's. A scared teenager doesn't think in literary metaphors. They think in the references a scared teenager would know.

 Read dialogue metaphors aloud in the character's voice. If the metaphor sounds wrong spoken aloud by this character, it sounds wrong on the page too.

 Quick self-diagnostic:

 Read a chapter and mark every metaphor. For each one, ask: would THIS character, with THEIR background, make THIS comparison? Any mismatch is an authorial intrusion.

 Before & After

 Fantasy

 Before
 Elena, a village healer's apprentice, watched the army approach and thought it looked like "a tsunami of steel, an inexorable tide of military might crashing against the shore of civilization."

 After
 Elena watched the army approach. It looked like a blight — the kind that starts at the edge of a field and eats inward until there's nothing green left. She'd treated blight. You couldn't treat this.

 A healer from a farming village compares the army to crop disease. The metaphor comes from HER world, and it reveals how she processes threat — as something that infects and destroys organic things.

 Thriller

 Before
 Twelve-year-old Mia described the abandoned house as "a sepulchral monument to entropy, its once-proud facade now a palimpsest of neglect and existential decay."

 After
 The house looked like someone had started to erase it and gotten bored halfway through. The front steps were gone. One window had cardboard instead of glass. The mailbox was still there, though, which somehow made it worse. Like the house was still expecting something.

 A twelve-year-old's metaphor: "started to erase it." Simple, visual, and exactly how a kid would process a decaying building. The mailbox detail is the kind of thing a child would fixate on.

 Watch Out

 Don't make every metaphor painfully on-the-nose. A soldier doesn't ONLY think in military terms. They're a whole person. But their metaphors should feel natural to their lived experience, which is broader than just their job.

 Don't dumb down metaphors for characters who aren't intellectuals. A farmer can be profoundly eloquent using agricultural imagery. A mechanic can be poetic about engines. Intelligence expresses through the character's domain, not the author's vocabulary.

 Pro tip

 The strongest character-voice metaphors surprise the reader with their aptness. When a blacksmith describes heartbreak as "the moment you pull metal from the forge and realize you've already ruined it — the shape is wrong and it's too late to reheat," the reader learns about heartbreak AND blacksmithing AND the character simultaneously. Triple duty from one comparison. That's what character-authentic metaphor can do.

## 原文 K｜Metaphor Source Domain（角色比喻来源域）

来源：https://loreteller.com/learn/fix-character-voice/

Metaphor Source Domain

 This is the dimension most writers miss entirely. Every person draws their figurative language from a specific domain of experience. A chef describes relationships in terms of ingredients and heat. A carpenter talks about foundations and load-bearing walls. A gambler frames everything as odds and stakes.

 When all your characters pull metaphors from the same source, they sound like the same person even if everything else differs. If your blacksmith and your poet both describe love as "a journey," neither one sounds like themselves.

 Patrick Rothfuss exploits this brilliantly in The Name of the Wind . Kvothe, a musician, describes everything through the language of music. Rhythm, harmony, dissonance. When he encounters beauty, it's a melody. When something goes wrong, it's a note played flat. His metaphor domain is so consistent that it becomes a defining signature of his narration.

Assign different metaphor domains. Go through your dialogue and highlight every comparison, every figurative expression. If two characters draw from the same well, reassign one. Let your former sailor think in tides and knots. Let your musician hear everything as pitch and tempo. The metaphor domain is like an accent for the imagination: it marks where a character's mind lives.

## 原文 M｜Simile Overload（明喻过载 / 像…链）

来源：https://fixyourfiction.com/mistakes/simile-overload/

Simile Overload

"The sword gleamed like moonlight. His eyes were like dark pools. She moved like a shark through water." Every other sentence compares something to something else.

Similes are cognitively expensive — they ask the reader to hold two images simultaneously. One per page is a gift. Four per paragraph is a tax.

Worst version: simile chains. "The pain was like fire. Like glass breaking. Like a scream frozen in amber." Pick one.

Why It Kills Your Fiction

Overload pulls readers from the scene. Every "like" forces a momentary exit into comparison-land. Once per page: enriching. Once per sentence: never immersed.

It signals the writer doesn't trust descriptions. If you can't describe a sword's gleam without moonlight, simile is a crutch.

Stacked similes cancel out. If pain is like fire AND glass AND frozen screams, the reader forms no image. They just know "it hurt."

The Fix

Budget 3-4 similes per chapter. Each earned, fresh, and the ONLY comparison in its paragraph. Replace rest with direct description.

Step-by-Step Repair

Search "like" and "as" in your manuscript. Highlight every simile. Count per chapter.

Keep only 3-4 strongest per chapter. Cut rest and replace with direct description.

Evaluate survivors: fresh? Surprising? "Eyes like pools" is dead. "Eyes like coins at a well's bottom" is specific.

Replace weak similes with sensory detail. Not "dark as night" but "the corridor ate her flashlight beam after ten feet."

Check for simile chains (multiple comparisons for one thing). Keep the best. Kill the rest.

Quick self-diagnostic:

Count similes per chapter. More than 6-8 is overload. More than 2 per paragraph is a red flag.

Before & After

Fantasy

Before
The tower rose like a finger at heaven. Walls like obsidian, smooth as glass, dark as a moonless night. Windows glowed like eyes watching like a predator.

After
The tower was obsidian — not like it, literally built from it. Black walls reflecting nothing. The windows were the only light, and they tracked her across the field.

Zero similes. Direct description is more immersive because the reader stays in the scene.

Thriller

Before
The crime scene was like hell. Blood spread like a carpet. Smell hit like a wall, sweet as rot, thick as fog.

After
Blood on the floor, enough to darken the carpet from beige to black. Marsh breathed through her mouth. It didn't help. The smell was already in her teeth.

"In her teeth" does more than three similes. Specific, visceral, original.

Romance

Before
His smile was like sunshine. Voice like honey, smooth as silk, warm as a blanket. Being near him felt like standing by fire.

After
He smiled, and she realized she'd been holding her breath. For how long, she didn't want to know.

One physiological response replaces a pile of cliche comparisons.

Watch Out

Don't eliminate all similes. A perfectly placed, original one is among fiction's greatest tools.

Don't replace similes with equally cliched description. "Piercing blue eyes" isn't better than "eyes like ice."

Similes in dialogue are natural and reveal character. Keep those.

Pro tip

Best similes compare across categories — abstract to concrete, visual to auditory. "The silence was thick enough to lean on." "Her grief had the weight of wet wool." Cross-sense comparisons create images nothing else can.

## 原文 N｜Metaphor Density / Stacking（叠喻 / 混喻）

来源：https://fixyourfiction.com/mistakes/metaphor-stacking/

Metaphor Density / Stacking

"Her heart was a fortress, walls built from stones of betrayal, moated by tears, guarded by dragons of distrust." One metaphor is powerful. Three in the same sentence is a traffic jam.

Stacking happens when the writer extends a comparison past its load-bearing capacity. Heart-as-fortress works. Adding a moat pushes it. Dragons break it.

Related: mixed metaphors. "Navigate the minefield of emotions, keeping head above water while building bridges." Minefield, ocean, or construction site?

Why It Kills Your Fiction

Each metaphor asks the reader to build an image. Stacking forces build, discard, rebuild in rapid succession. Cognitive overload kills immersion.

Extended metaphors have diminishing returns. By the time you add dragons, the reader forgot the original point.

Mixed metaphors create impossible images. Minefields and oceans in the same sentence — visual brain throws an error.

The Fix

One metaphor per paragraph maximum. Let it land. If extending, commit to one comparison fully.

Step-by-Step Repair

Highlight every metaphor and simile per chapter. Flag paragraphs with more than one.

For multi-metaphor paragraphs, keep the strongest. Cut the rest.

Check extended metaphors. Past two sentences, probably overextending.

Search for mixed metaphors where comparisons clash. Pick one lane.

Read metaphor-heavy passages to someone. If they laugh, the metaphor is absurd.

Quick self-diagnostic:

Find your most metaphor-dense paragraph. Count distinct comparisons. 3+ in one paragraph means stacking.

Before & After

Fantasy

Before
Her heart was a fortress of ice, walls built from frozen tears, behind crystalline ramparts the fire of rage burned like a nova in the void of shattered trust.

After
Her heart was a fortress. She'd made sure of that. And she hadn't left a gate.

One metaphor, three sentences. "No gate" extends perfectly without new images.

Thriller

Before
Marsh navigated the minefield of politics while keeping her head above water in the sea of bureaucracy, building bridges with the brass who held the keys.

After
Departmental politics was less a minefield and more a dinner party where everyone had a gun and nobody knew who brought the bullets.

One committed metaphor. Dinner-party-with-guns is specific and sustained.

Romance

Before
Love was a garden she'd stopped tending, a ship she'd let drift, a fire gone cold, a book she'd closed and shelved.

After
Love was a garden she'd stopped tending. Weeds had taken the whole thing. She didn't even walk past it anymore.

One metaphor, fully committed. Weeds and avoidance tell everything.

Watch Out

Don't avoid metaphor entirely. One precise metaphor is immensely powerful.

Don't extend just because it's working. When it starts feeling clever rather than natural, stop.

Don't mix sensory domains in one sentence. Fire and water imagery together creates static.

Pro tip

The best single metaphor does everything a stack tries to do. "Fortress with no gate" — guarded, deliberate, permanent. Three metaphors' worth in one image. If your metaphor needs reinforcement, the first wasn't strong enough.

## 原文 O｜Whom Does The Metaphor Serve?（比喻为谁服务 / 文化语境）

Whom Does The Metaphor Serve? — Victor Manibo
来源：https://www.victormanibo.com/blog/whom-does-your-metaphor-serve
（文中引用 Matthew Salesses《Craft in the Real World》练习原文）

A metaphor is where a character meets the world—a specific character and a specific world—and translates the experience for the audience. An Asian American character comparing chopsticks to two snakes in her hand, for example, is not for an Asian American audience. Whom does the metaphor serve? What exactly is it translating? Go through your manuscript and interrogate each metaphor for whether it is relevant to the character, world, and audience. What shared context is necessary for the metaphor to work? Does it do the job better than a concrete description would?

For example, one of my scenes involves a story about an aging film star, and an early draft contained this simile:

The director orders everyone to a break. Beatriz leaves the set, turning to her assistant to ask for some ice water. The director, ears like a fox, vetoes the request. "You're supposed to be protecting your vocal chords," he says.

“Ears like a fox” requires a shared context between me and the reader/audience, of knowing what a fox is and how they are known for their keen sense of hearing. That audience however is limited, and much of the word would not have the necessary context for the comparison. Indeed, in the Philippines, where this story is set, there are no foxes; and these Filipino characters, though they may know what a fox is, are not likely to attribute good hearing to a fox, but rather some other native animal, like a deer. A fox is simply not relevant to this world and these characters, and is only relevant to an audience familiar with the fox (and that overused comparison).

Just goes to show how I do have some habits to break. More than thoughtfully picking similes and metaphors that aren’t cliches, I need to be more aware of what cultural contexts I’ve unconsciously absorbed, and more aware of whose cultural contexts my writing affirms and negates.
